CONTEXT

About the learner

  • Level: entering Grade 3 (Québec Cycle 2). The program is rich — place value to the ten-thousands, all four operations, fractions, decimals, geometry, measurement, data and probability.
  • Ability: capable and quick — please pitch to his real level, not an easy level. He thrives on a genuine but reachable challenge, and gets bored by drill that's too easy.
  • Motivation: loves games, quick wins, points, and understanding why. Keep sessions lively and hands-on; short and focused beats long.
  • Goal: a confident head start so class feels like review, plus fluency in the two things Grade 3 leans on hardest — place value and times tables.
✅ What works best: use a concrete model first (blocks, arrays, a number line), then the numbers, then a word problem. After a correct answer, ask “how do you know?” — explaining the reasoning out loud is what builds real understanding.

SECTION 4

How to run a 30–40 minute session

  1. Warm-up (3 min): quick-fire times tables or “what's the value of the 7 in 3,742?”
  2. Model it (5 min): show the idea with a concrete model — blocks, coins, an array drawn on paper, a number line.
  3. Teach the numbers (7 min): connect the model to the written math. Show one clean worked example, then do a second one together.
  4. Practice (10 min): he does the lesson's exercises, then a quiz round on the practice page. You watch for the pattern in any errors.
  5. Word problem & close (5–8 min): one real-life word problem using today's skill, then he explains his method back to you (his “exit ticket”).
🎯 Concrete → pictorial → abstract, in that order. Most Grade 3 struggles come from jumping to the written algorithm before the idea is solid with objects or pictures.
KEY TIP

💪 Give him LOTS of sample questions

At this age, volume and repetition matter enormously. For every skill, give him far more problems than a single lesson holds. The goal is for him to see the same idea in dozens of forms until it's automatic.

Where to get questions

  • The practice page — a 98-question bank across 16 topics. He can replay each topic as often as he likes; questions are drawn at random.
  • The lesson pack — worked exercises with answer keys for every topic.
  • His workbook and everyday numbers around the house.

How to make more in 10 seconds

  • Change the numbers: keep the structure, swap the digits (23 × 4 → 34 × 2; 265 + 137 → 254 + 128).
  • Have HIM write them: ask him to make up 3 problems of the same type for you to solve — inventing is learning twice.
  • Use real life: prices, clock times, sharing snacks, measuring — turn them into quick word problems.
  • Vary the format: oral quick-fire, written, “spot my mistake”, true/false, fill-in-the-blank.
✅ Aim for 10–15 questions per skill each session, mixed easy → hard, and revisit the skill across several days (spaced practice) rather than one big dose.
SECTION 5

Deep teaching notes & common misconceptions

Place value — the keystone

Everything downstream (regrouping, big multiplication, decimals) depends on this. Insist he can say the value of a digit, not just name it: in 4,231 the 2 isn't “two”, it's “two hundred.” Use base-ten blocks or bundle straws.

⚠️ Misconception: reading 3,051 as “three hundred fifty-one” (dropping the zero's job as a place-holder). Fix with a place-value chart — the 0 holds the hundreds place.

Regrouping (carrying / borrowing)

Name what's really happening: carrying = “10 ones become 1 ten”; borrowing = “1 ten becomes 10 ones.” Do it with blocks once before the written method.

⚠️ Misconception in subtraction: doing “bigger minus smaller” in each column regardless of order (e.g. 52 − 27 → “5−2 and 7−2”). Always subtract top minus bottom; regroup when the top is smaller.

Multiplication & the times tables

Teach × as equal groups and as an array (rows × columns) — the array also shows why 4×6 = 6×4. Learn tables in this smart order: 2, 5, 10 (easy) → 4 (double the 2s) → 3 → 9 (finger trick / digits add to 9) → squares → 6, 7, 8 last. Fact families link × and ÷: 6×7=42, so 42÷7=6.

💡 The only “hard” facts once 0,1,2,5,10 are known are a handful: 3×6, 3×7, 3×8, 4×6, 4×7, 4×8, 6×7, 6×8, 7×8. Drill those, not all 100.

Fractions

A fraction is equal parts of one whole. Stress “equal.” The bottom (denominator) says how many equal parts the whole is cut into; the top (numerator) says how many you take. Use paper folding and fraction strips.

⚠️ Misconception: “bigger bottom number = bigger fraction.” 1/8 < 1/4 because eighths are smaller pieces. Compare with a drawn strip, not just the numbers.

Decimals & money

Money is the friendliest doorway: $3.50 = 3 dollars and 5 dimes = 3 and 5 tenths. Line up the decimal point when adding/subtracting. Connect 0.5 = 1/2 = 50¢.

⚠️ Reminder: his school writes decimals with a comma (« 3,5 »). Say explicitly that 3,5 (school) = 3.5 (here) so he isn't thrown in class.

Geometry & measurement

Keep it hands-on: fold paper for symmetry, walk the classroom to spot parallel/perpendicular lines and right angles, count squares for area, walk the edge for perimeter. Perimeter = around the edge; area = the space inside (in squares). These two are the most-confused pair — always contrast them together.

SECTION 6

The 6 models that unlock Grade 3 math

  1. Base-ten blocks (or bundled straws/coins) — place value, regrouping, big +/−.
  2. Number line — counting on, rounding, patterns, and later fractions/decimals.
  3. Arrays (dots in rows & columns) — multiplication, the commutative link, and area.
  4. Fraction strips / folded paper — fractions, comparing, equivalence.
  5. Money (coins & bills) — decimals, place value, real word problems.
  6. Grid paper — area, perimeter, the coordinate plane, symmetry.

You don't need a store-bought kit — coins, dried beans, folded paper and grid paper cover all six.
